Physics behind the simulator

The plant — SLX-100

The engine runs a compact pressurized water reactor named SLX-100 (Single-Loop eXperimental): about 100 MWe / 300 MWt. Primary coolant stays liquid under high pressure; heat crosses a steam generator into a separate secondary loop that drives the turbine-generator. The two loops never mix.

Layout. One reactor coolant loop — core, one reactor coolant pump, one U-tube steam generator, and a pressurizer on the hot leg — plus the secondary plant (main steam, turbine-generator, condenser, feedwater) and the usual support systems (chemical and volume control, emergency injection, residual heat removal, protection). That is the whole plant on the board: small on purpose, so heatup, power operation, and casualties all sit on one continuous model.

Energy path. Fission heat → primary coolant (hot leg → steam-generator tubes → cold leg → pump → core) → secondary steam → turbine → electrical output. The pressurizer holds primary pressure with heaters, spray, and relief. Steam demand on the secondary is what the primary “feels” as load.

At hot full power the primary runs near 2235 psi (15.41 MPa) and average coolant temperature near 579 °F (304 °C), with roughly 74 °F (41 °C) of subcooling margin. Modes run from cold shutdown through full power on the same physics.

The sections below describe how that plant is computed in real time — training fidelity, not a licensing code or a twin of a named commercial unit.

Model in short: one average core power (point kinetics) with delayed neutrons · lumped heat and mass balances · steam demand coupled to the primary · protection that reads instruments · accident regimes left open · no spatial core map.

Core power and reactivity

  • Point kinetics — the core is one average power level, not a 3-D flux map. Delayed neutrons (six groups) set how fast power can rise or fall, so reactor period and startup rate are real quantities on the nuclear instruments.
  • What moves power: control rods, fuel temperature (Doppler), coolant density, dissolved boron, and xenon. Moderator feedback follows density, not a single fixed temperature coefficient.
  • Xenon builds and burns on iodine/xenon timescales after power changes and after a trip. The model tracks whole-core poison only — not local peaking or spatial oscillation.
  • Decay heat continues after a scram and depends on how long the core was at power. A freshly started core and a post-trip core are different heat sources even with rods fully in.

Heat, pressure, and inventory

  • Lumped thermal model — a few nodes (fuel, cladding, coolant, secondary) exchange heat with simple transfer laws. Enough to get the operator-visible temperatures and rates right; not a full fluid-dynamics code.
  • Pressurizer — a steam bubble, heaters, spray, and relief. Level moves with inventory and thermal expansion. The plant can go solid (full of water) or empty the bubble — those ends are allowed.
  • Coolant inventory — charging, letdown, safety injection, accumulators, leaks, and relief all change mass. Subcooling is tracked; the primary can void; pumps can cavitate if suction margin is lost.
  • Hard cases stay open. Steam-generator tubes that uncover transfer less heat; a voided primary and a solid pressurizer are reachable states. Those regimes are where many of the training accidents live — the model does not clamp them away.

Steam side and generator

  • Primary heat boils secondary water. Steam flow, feedwater, dumps, and isolation share one balance, so operator actions on the secondary change how heat leaves the core.
  • Turbine, condenser, and generator close the loop. Electrical load is a steam demand: raise or lower it and the primary feels it. Lose the turbine or the heat sink and the stored energy still has to go somewhere.

Instruments and protection

Gauges are instrument channels — lagged, and able to stick or drift when failures are injected. Reactor trips, interlocks, safeguards, and automatic controllers use those channel readings, not hidden “true” plant values. A bad transmitter can mislead both you and the automation.

What is left out on purpose

  • No spatial core — no 3-D flux shape or hot-spot map.
  • One loop, lumped nodes — not multi-loop detail or subchannel thermal-hydraulics.
  • No full containment / LOCA engineering suite — core-damage paths go far enough to teach progression, not licensing analysis.
  • Not a named plant twin — a composite ~100 MWe plant built so ordinary operator-visible dynamics behave right.

Detail is spent where you feel it on the board. For coefficients, step order, and declared departures, see the simulation-physics chapter in the in-sim manual, or the engine sources on GitHub (open source, AGPL).
