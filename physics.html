Physics behind the simulator

What the model actually computes, what its numbers are anchored to, and what is deliberately left out. Written for readers with a science or engineering background; the operator-facing tour is on the About page.

Reactor Dynamics is a real-time, lumped-parameter model of a single-loop pressurized water reactor of roughly 100 MWe. The whole plant — core, primary loop, pressurizer, steam generator, turbine-generator, and the control and protection systems — advances as one coupled system in sub-second time steps, in real time or under acceleration. It is built for training, not licensing. The standard it aims at: every transient an operator sees should be explainable with ordinary reactor-plant physics, and wherever the model is pinned to a number, that number should come from published plant data rather than intuition.

Core and reactivity

  • Point kinetics, six delayed-neutron groups, with standard U-235 group constants (βeff ≈ 0.65%). Reactor period and startup rate fall out of the kinetics and drive the source- and intermediate-range nuclear instrumentation, so approaching criticality is a real exercise in rate control, not a scripted event.
  • Reactivity feedbacks: Doppler (fuel temperature), moderator feedback that tracks coolant density rather than a constant temperature coefficient, dissolved boron, and xenon.
  • Xenon as an explicit decay chain — I-135 → Xe-135 with their real half-lives (6.6 h and 9.1 h): production, decay, and flux burnout. Xenon transients after power changes and after a trip behave on the timescales they do in a real core.

The reactivity parameters are fitted to measurements, not chosen. Rod bank worths are the measured values from a Westinghouse training-simulator dataset (4068 pcm control banks, 3676 pcm shutdown banks). The moderator coefficients are least-squares fits to the three measured hot-zero-power isothermal temperature coefficients published in MIT’s BEAVRS benchmark (−1.75 pcm/°F at 975 ppm boron, −4.65 at 902, −8.01 at 810), with residuals under 0.1 pcm/°F. Core excess reactivity is then solved so that hot-zero-power, all-rods-out critical boron lands on BEAVRS’s measured 975 ppm.

Thermal-hydraulics

  • Lumped two-node core thermal model — a fuel node and a coolant node with first-order heat transfer between them and onward to the steam generator. Decay heat is a separate source term that persists after shutdown: a scrammed core is still a heat source, and the plant has to deal with it.
  • The pressurizer couples RCS inventory, thermal expansion, and steam-space thermodynamics, with heaters, spray, and relief valves acting on it. Its geometry is taken from a real plant’s final safety analysis report — the steam space is 5.8% of RCS volume, per the Beaver Valley Unit 2 UFSAR — so a given insurge or outsurge moves indicated level the way that geometry says it should.
  • Degraded regimes are modeled, not fenced off. Subcooling margin is tracked; steam generator heat transfer collapses as the tube bundle uncovers; the primary can void, and the pressurizer can go solid. The interesting accidents live in these regimes, so the model has to as well.

Secondary plant and electrical

  • Primary heat boils secondary water; steam flow, feedwater (main and auxiliary), and steam generator level form one continuous mass-energy balance, with dumps and isolation valves changing where the heat goes.
  • Turbine, condenser, and generator close the loop. Electrical load is a steam demand: change load and the primary feels it through steam generator pressure and temperature. Condenser performance depends on circulating water temperature. Trip the turbine or lose the heat sink and the stored energy has to go somewhere — the model makes you decide where.

Instrumentation and protection

  • Every reading on the board is a modeled channel, not a window onto the state vector: first-order lag and channel noise, with injectable failure modes — stuck, drifting, or noisy — plus component-level failures like a relief valve that will not close and an indicator that reports it shut anyway.
  • Trips, interlocks, engineered safeguards, and the automatic controllers act only on instrument channels. Nothing in the protection system can see the true state. A failed sensor therefore degrades the machinery’s judgment exactly as it degrades yours, which is the point.

How we know it behaves

  • Sourced, not recalled. Plant numbers — setpoints, worths, volumes, coefficients — change only with a citation to a primary document: utility final safety analysis reports, NRC standard technical specifications, and measured benchmark data such as BEAVRS. The citations are recorded in the repository alongside the changes.
  • Gated by test, on every change: an aggregate gate of 30-odd suites and several thousand automated checks — physics acceptance scenarios, a plant behavior catalog, full procedure replays through the complete control stack, and reachability checks that every trip setpoint lies inside the range its instrument can physically indicate.
  • Open source. The model is not a black box: the code is AGPL-licensed and on GitHub. If you want to know exactly what an equation does, read it.

What is deliberately left out

  • No spatial core. Point kinetics means no 3-D flux map, no nodal power distribution. Where a real crew manages power shape, this plant does not ask them to.
  • One loop, lumped nodes. No subchannel thermal-hydraulics; thermal limits are lumped indicators rather than hot-channel calculations.
  • No containment engineering suite. Core-damage sequences exist and are modeled far enough to teach the progression — not to analyze it.
  • Not a digital twin of any named unit. It is a composite plant anchored to published data from several real ones, which is why no single unit will match it exactly.

The rule throughout: detail is spent where the operator feels it, and the rest is simplified on purpose. Where a simplification understates reality, the in-sim manual says so plainly.
