Physics behind the simulator

Plant dynamics and the physics that drive them — the why behind what you see when you operate the plant. For science or engineering readers. Operating the plant (the what) is on About and in the control room.

In brief: six-group point kinetics · lumped primary and secondary · protection driven from board channels · parameters fitted to published plant data · no spatial core · not a named digital twin.

The model is a real-time, lumped-parameter single-loop pressurized water reactor of roughly 100 MWe. Core, primary, pressurizer, steam generator, turbine-generator, and control/protection advance as one coupled system in sub-second steps (real time or accelerated).

Training, not licensing. We aim for operator-visible dynamics that match ordinary plant physics. Where the model uses a plant number — a setpoint, a rod worth, a tank volume, a coefficient — it should come from published plant data, not a freehand guess.

Core and reactivity

  • Point kinetics — the core is treated as one average power level (no spatial flux map). Prompt neutrons would make power jump almost instantly; delayed neutrons, tracked in six groups with standard fission-product timescales, slow the response so reactor period and startup rate are real quantities. Those rates drive the source- and intermediate-range nuclear instruments, so going critical is about controlling how fast power rises, not a scripted jump to “critical.”
  • Feedbacks: Doppler (fuel temperature), moderator feedback from coolant density (not a single fixed temperature coefficient), dissolved boron, and xenon.
  • Xenon as I-135 → Xe-135 with production, decay, and flux burnout on their half-lives (as coded). After power changes and after a trip, poison moves on those timescales. There is no spatial peaking (see limits below).

Reactivity parameters are fitted to measurements, not chosen. Rod bank worths use a Westinghouse training-simulator dataset (4068 pcm control, 3676 pcm shutdown — as in the live config). Moderator coefficients are least-squares fits to three measured hot-zero-power isothermal temperature coefficients from MIT’s BEAVRS benchmark (−1.75 pcm/°F at 975 ppm boron, −4.65 at 902, −8.01 at 810). Core excess reactivity is solved so hot-zero-power, all-rods-out critical boron lands on BEAVRS’s measured 975 ppm.

Thermal-hydraulics

  • Two-node core thermal model — fuel and coolant nodes, first-order heat transfer to the steam generator. Decay heat continues after shutdown; a scrammed core is still a heat source.
  • Pressurizer — inventory, thermal expansion, and steam-space thermodynamics with heaters, spray, and relief. Steam-space share of RCS volume is taken from Beaver Valley Unit 2 UFSAR (5.8%), so insurge/outsurge move level with that geometry.
  • Degraded regimes stay open. Subcooling is tracked; SG heat transfer falls as tubes uncover; primary can void; pressurizer can go solid. Those regimes are where the hard training cases live.

Secondary plant and electrical

  • Primary heat boils secondary water. Steam, main/aux feed, and SG level share one mass-energy balance; dumps and isolation change where heat goes.
  • Turbine, condenser, and generator close the loop. Load is a steam demand — change it and the primary feels it. Lose the turbine or the heat sink and stored energy has to go somewhere.

Instrumentation and protection

Board readings are instrument channels (lag, noise; optional stuck or drift). Trips, interlocks, safeguards, and automatic controllers use those channel values as inputs.

How we know it behaves

  • Sourced, not recalled. Setpoints, worths, volumes, and coefficients change with a citation to a primary document (FSARs, NRC standard technical specifications, benchmarks such as BEAVRS), recorded in the repo with the change.
  • Automated gates cover physics scenarios, behavior checks, and procedure replays through the control stack. The suite is in the repository and changes with the plant.
  • Open source (AGPL) on GitHub — the equations are in the engine files.

What is deliberately left out

  • No spatial core — no 3-D flux map or nodal power shape.
  • One loop, lumped nodes — no subchannel TH; thermal limits are lumped, not hot-channel.
  • No full containment engineering suite — core-damage paths go far enough to teach progression, not licensing analysis.
  • Not a digital twin of a named unit — composite plant from published data; no single plant will match line for line.

Detail goes where the operator feels it; the rest is simplified on purpose. Where that understates reality, the in-sim manual says so.
